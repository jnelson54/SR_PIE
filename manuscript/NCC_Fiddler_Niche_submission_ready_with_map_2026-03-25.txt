Abstract
     Climate change can enable range expansion only when abiotic conditions and energy supply both support persistence. Using 25 years of high-tide estuarine hydrography (2000–2025) and an annual native-consumer stable-isotope time series from 2009–2020 at the Plum Island Ecosystems Long-Term Ecological Research site in the Gulf of Maine, we tested whether physical change and food-web rewiring preceded the 2014 arrival of the mud fiddler crab Minuca pugnax. Growing-season waters warmed in the lower and middle estuary (~0.8 °C per decade), and conductivity increased in the lower estuary (+1.20 mS cm−1 per decade; q = 0.0015). Over the same annual native-consumer series, marine plankton support increased from 19% to 47% in the lower estuary and from 31% to 59% in the middle estuary. Separate MixSIAR estimates from mud fiddler crabs sampled in 2023 and 2025 showed dominant support from marine plankton (73.4% ± 2.5%).
Introduction
     Climate change is reshaping the physical template of ecosystems, altering temperatures, salinity regimes, and oxygen dynamics across the globe. As those abiotic baselines shift, species ranges are reorganizing as warm-adapted taxa push into newly available habitats and cold-adapted species retreat1–3. Although temperature often frames explanations of range expansion under climate change, we know range expansions do not proceed uniformly and are filtered by physical and ecological processes4–7. Our modern understanding of Hutchinson’s niche concept would tell us a species' ability to expand its range is carved jointly by the physical template and the energetic landscape8,9. Abiotic regimes, classically referred to as the scenopoetic (e.g. temperature, salinity, oxygen, and hydroperiod), set the physical conditions that regulate the physiological constraints on a species’ range8–10. Simultaneously, bionomic axes, the consumable resources required to sustain species, determine when and where enough energy is available to meet metabolic demands of a species8,11. Populations persist only when energy intake from accessible resources reliably exceeds the costs imposed by the environment; when either side fails, ranges are limited. Climate change can push both sides: it widens or narrows scenopoetic limits (e.g. temperature, salinity) and reroutes the bionomic supply of usable energy through food webs. Thus, it can simultaneously open habitat and rewire resource availability. Classic examples show that establishment depends on context: warming of the East Australian Current allowed the long-spined sea urchin (Centrostephanus rodgersii) to reach Tasmanian reefs, but where predatory rock lobsters had been depleted the urchins established and overgrazed kelp, whereas intact predation damped impacts12. Conversely, abiotic extremes can reverse gains: black mangroves (Avicennia germinans) expand with fewer hard freezes along the temperate–subtropical ecotone, but severe cold snaps force diebacks13. These cases underscore a general rule: range entry is sustained only when scenopoetic thresholds are crossed and maintained, and when the energy pathway the migrant exploits remains dominant across critical seasons. These joint constraints are especially relevant in estuaries, where shelf-water exchange, river flow, and marsh inundation jointly restructure both habitat conditions and the delivery of organic matter to consumers.
     In 2014, researchers documented the presence of the mud fiddler crab (Minuca pugnax) in the salt marshes of the Plum Island Long-Term Ecological Research Site (PIE LTER)1. This was the first observation of this species north of Boston and the first burrowing crab in Plum Island since at least the last glaciation1. The historic range of the mud fiddler crab along the U.S. east coast extended across the entire temperate region from Cape Cod to the central Florida peninsula, with populations throughout the northern and central Gulf of Mexico. They can be quite prolific with burrow densities more than a hundred per square meter14. The mud fiddler crab is an ecosystem engineer whose burrowing activity can influence geomorphic and ecological processes in coastal marshes from carbon sequestration to plant growth and physiology15–18. Adults feed on deposited organic material and associated benthic particles around their burrows, although the information on diet composition is somewhat limited18. Studying how their expansion alters the function of the PIE ecosystem will be an important question for future research. However, the co-location of a novel range expansion into a well-studied LTER provides an unprecedented opportunity to ask if joint scenopoetic and bionomic niche thresholds were crossed to allow for the expansion of the mud fiddler crab beyond its historic range.
     Here we use 25 years of high-tide sonde observations of estuarine temperature and conductivity (2000–2025) and an annual native-consumer stable-isotope analysis of food-web structure from 2009–2020 to test whether changes in the physical template and energetic landscape preceded the expansion of the mud fiddler crab. We then compare those native-consumer trends with separate mud fiddler crab MixSIAR estimates from 2023 and 2025. Throughout, upper, middle, and lower PIE refer to the landward-to-seaward estuarine gradient shown in Fig. 1, from freshwater-influenced creeks to marine-dominated reaches. Specifically, we pair long-term anomalies and trends in temperature and salinity with independent isotope-based indicators of energy flow through the PIE food web across regions. We test whether range expansion is enabled by coordinated shifts along two niche axes. First, warming increases the seasonal window of suitable temperatures, releasing a scenopoetic constraint. Second, salinity increase signals enhanced marine forcing. Third, strengthened marine forcing and sea-level rise increase marine-derived subsidies to benthic consumers, shifting energy pathways toward a planktonic POM-based production pathway and expanding the bionomic axis. If both axes shift concurrently, establishment should transition to persistence and rapid spread.
Results
Changes in estuarine physical parameters
     Long-term high-tide hydrography from PIE LTER sondes indicates sustained warming of estuarine waters coincident with the establishment and expansion of the mud fiddler crab. We analyzed continuous sondes spanning the lower and middle estuary (2000–2025) and summarized temperature as seasonal (spring, summer, fall) anomalies after removing the long-term seasonal mean at each station. Temperature anomalies increased significantly in both regions (Fig. 2), corresponding to warming rates of 0.79 °C decade−1 in the lower estuary (95% CI 0.38–1.21; q = 6 × 10−4) and 0.82 °C decade−1 in the middle estuary (0.46–1.15; q = 5.4 × 10−4). Early years (2000–2013) were dominated by negative anomalies, whereas anomalies were persistently positive after 2014 (mean post-2014 anomaly: +0.60 °C lower, +0.71 °C middle), indicating a shift toward systematically warmer growing-season conditions.
     Conductivity (a proxy for salinity) anomalies also tended to be higher in recent years (Fig. 2). Conductivity increased significantly in the lower estuary (+1.20 mS cm−1 decade−1; 95% CI 0.25–2.21; q = 0.0015), while the middle-estuary trend was positive but not distinguishable from zero (+2.31 mS cm−1 decade−1; q = 0.121). Together, these sonde records indicate a sustained shift toward warmer and, in the seaward reach, more marine-influenced conditions during the growing season.
Freshwater discharge
     USGS daily discharge from Parker River at Byfield, MA (site 01101000) showed a modest but significant decline in monthly discharge anomalies during March–November from 2000–2025 (−0.116 m3 s−1 decade−1; q = 0.0037; Supplementary Fig. 1). Annual mean discharge also trended downward (−0.171 m3 s−1 decade−1), but that annual trend was not distinguishable from zero (q = 0.199). Pre-2014 versus post-2014 monthly means were lower after 2014 by 0.316 m3 s−1 overall (Wilcoxon p = 0.058; t-test p = 0.036), with the largest monthly decline in June (−0.666 m3 s−1; Wilcoxon p = 0.011). These discharge records indicate somewhat lower river input during the same season used for the hydrographic analysis, so reduced freshwater input may have contributed to higher salinity. However, the discharge analysis alone does not identify the dominant driver of the sustained conductivity increase, which we therefore interpret primarily as evidence consistent with stronger marine influence rather than as a discharge-only signal.
Fiddler crab burrow counts
     The mud fiddler crab population in PIE has grown rapidly since 2014, when initial surveys found burrows in ~50% of transects; by 2019 and onward, 100% of transect locations contained burrows. Burrow density also increased steadily, with across-creek annual medians of ~30–32 burrows m−2 in 2023–2024 and 44 burrows m−2 in 2025 (Fig. 3). A log-linear trend fitted to across-creek annual medians showed a ~40% increase per year (estimate = 0.340 yr−1 on log scale; 40.5% yr−1, 95% CI 19.9–64.6%; q = 0.0015), and a non-parametric check confirmed a strong monotonic rise (Kendall’s tau = 0.76, q = 0.0023; Supplementary Table 1). Together, these results indicate that densities increased several-fold over the decade even after occupancy reached 100% at all transect points.
Increasing marine plankton support of PIE food webs
     Marine plankton (POM) contributions to native consumer biomass increased over the 2009–2020 annual time series, with strong regional structure (Fig. 4). The combined food-web annual median marine plankton contribution increased in the lower and middle estuary but remained near zero in the upper estuary. Median percent contribution of plankton increased from 19% to 47% in the lower estuary and from 31% to 59% in the middle estuary over the annual native-consumer series (Fig. 4a). After Benjamini-Hochberg correction, the middle-estuary regional trend remained significant, whereas the lower-estuary regional trend was positive but fell just above the adjusted threshold. Species-level slopes were also generally positive for blue mussels, silversides, and softshell clams, but no single species trend remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Fig. 4b). Mummichogs showed little directional change, maintaining a mean contribution of ~33% across the study period.
     To quantify resource-pathway support for the expanding species in PIE, we applied the same isotope mixing model to mud fiddler crabs using a separate set of 20 individuals sampled in 2023 and 2025. Mud fiddler crab biomass was estimated to be supported by 73.4% ± 2.5% marine plankton (POM), 25.9% ± 2.7% Spartina, and 0.7% ± 0.7% benthic microalgae, positioning the species as strongly coupled to the same plankton-supported pathway that increased in importance for the resident consumer assemblage (Fig. 4, green reference band).
Discussion
     Our results fit a broader pattern in which global change asymmetrically rewires food webs by differentially altering habitats and the energy pathways that connect them. In PIE, regional ocean forcing strengthened the marine plankton pathway into marsh networks, while spring warming and salinification crossed physiological and habitat thresholds for a warm-affinity burrower. Conceptually, this is the coastal analog of asymmetric rewiring observed across ecosystems: when one compartment (here, shelf waters) is altered more than others, generalist consumers re-weight habitat coupling and energy flows reorganize19. Similar reweighting follows marine heatwaves and thermal anomalies that reshape plankton composition and carbon processing with cascading consumer effects20. From these dynamics follow testable predictions: (i) years with earlier, saltier springs and sustained marine-plankton access should show the largest step-changes in consumer diets; (ii) responses should be region-stratified, strongest at inland margins where saline intrusion expands the accessible niche; and (iii) colonization success of warm-affinity migrants should covary with the magnitude and persistence of marine-pathway dominance. Tracking these indicators in integrated monitoring offers early warning of impending rewiring and range entries in other rapidly warming, sea-level-rise estuaries.
      The Gulf of Maine (GoM) has entered a distinctly warmer, more variable regime driven by shifts in the balance between Gulf Stream-derived Atlantic Water and the Labrador Current21. Syntheses show multi-decadal warming with marked acceleration in the last decade, including unusually strong summertime incursions of warm, salty water onto the shelf, and basin-scale marine heatwaves (2012, 2016)22,23. These forcings lengthen the warm season, increase stratification, and advance springlike conditions. The same signatures appear in PIE as a mid-2010s shift to warmer springs and stronger saline influence within the estuary, conditions consistent with enhanced shelf influence (Fig. 2).
     GoM-scale biological and physical changes foreshadow PIE’s trophic reorganization. Warming and altered mixing have re-sorted shelf communities toward temperate taxa; projections indicate continued surface warming, saltier depths, and stronger stratification, conditions that enhance delivery and residence of marine-sourced plankton in coastal corridors. Our stable isotopes track a decadal rise in marine-plankton support for consumers in lower and middle PIE, while upper-estuary responses remain muted, exactly the gradient expected from the inland propagation of saline influence. Analogous threshold dynamics underpin the recent lobster boom (juvenile thermal gate ≈ 12 °C interacting with local mixing), and centuries-long, now-accelerating sea-level rise amplifies tidal prisms and inland saline penetration, pushing marine signals farther into marsh networks and increasing particle delivery to feeding zones of the mud fiddler crab.
     This interpretation does not require adult mud fiddler crabs to graze directly on suspended phytoplankton in the water column. Instead, the marine plankton (POM) isotope signal likely reaches the marsh surface through deposited suspended particles and trophic processing within benthic food webs, where plankton-derived organic matter can be incorporated into surface sediments, microbes, meiofauna, and other deposit-feeding resources available to adult crabs. Our isotope analysis therefore identifies coupling to a plankton-supported basal pathway, not direct filtration of water-column phytoplankton by adults. Larval use of offshore planktonic resources may also contribute to lifetime isotopic composition, but the adult marsh food web provides the most direct ecological linkage to persistence within PIE.
     Together these forces reach PIE along three linked pathways that match our signals. (i) Boundary-condition shift: earlier, saltier shelf inflow drives spring warming and salinification, with a lagged step inland as intrusion propagates. (ii) Energetic rerouting: strengthened shelf influence and stratification increase plankton-derived organic matter in the estuary, captured as rising POM contribution to consumer biomass in lower and middle PIE. (iii) Tidal amplification: sea-level rise increases flood extent and particle transport onto marsh platforms. The result is an asymmetric rewiring: marine pathways strengthen relative to riverine and detrital ones while the scenopoetic gate (spring temperature and salinity) opens, explaining the timing (mid-2010s entry), spatial gradient (seaward first, interior lag), and rapid post-entry growth we observe. Alternative influences (e.g. episodic larval supply, freshwater-discharge anomalies, geomorphic change) remain plausible, but the coherent directionality and regional synchrony across physical and trophic indicators point to GoM forcing as the dominant driver.
     Published experiments on mud fiddler crab larvae show that successful development to the megalopa stage is strongly temperature dependent: few larvae complete development at temperatures below ~18 °C, and no larvae complete development at 14 °C or 10 °C. Oceanographic data from near the historical northern range limit indicate that summer sea-surface temperatures historically exceeded 16 °C only briefly each year, and temperatures at 5–23 m depth, where later-stage larvae are commonly found, were often <16 °C even in midsummer. These constraints led Sanford et al.25 to infer a lower larval thermal gate around 18 °C, which, together with a short summer warm period, historically limited northward expansion.
     Our anomaly analysis indicates that the PIE estuary has warmed sufficiently that spring and early-summer conditions in the middle and upper estuary are now consistently 1–2 °C warmer than the pre-2014 baseline. When placed in the context of the Massachusetts Bay and Gulf of Maine records, where 2012–2013 summers were 0.7–1.3 °C warmer than the 2001–2013 mean during the period when the mud fiddler crab expanded northward, the magnitude of the PIE anomalies is comparable. In practical terms, this warming lengthens the seasonal window in which near-surface and shallow-shelf waters exceed the ~18 °C larval threshold, and reduces the frequency with which larvae experience development-limiting temperatures (<14–16 °C) during their offshore phase.
     Thus, the hydrographic changes we document, multi-decadal warming of ~0.8 °C per decade and a shift from predominantly negative to consistently positive spring anomalies, are consistent with relaxation of the larval thermal barrier identified by Sanford et al.25 Within this expanded thermal window, the concurrent increase in estuarine salinity and the strengthening of plankton-supported energy pathways act as complementary scenopoetic and bionomic changes that support the establishment and rapid expansion of the mud fiddler crab in PIE.
     Our results emphasize that maintenance, not just threshold crossing, governs establishment. Operationally, two indicators travel well across systems: (a) a persistence metric for the scenopoetic gate (e.g. multi-year exceedance of a spring temperature or salinity threshold); and (b) a dominance metric for the bionomic axis (e.g. sustained rise in plankton-supported contribution to consumers). Transient expansions are expected when either indicator fails, for example hard-freeze years that reset mangrove limits despite overall warming, or strong top-down control that suppresses urchin expansion even as currents warm.
Methods
Plum Island Ecosystems (PIE) LTER, Massachusetts, USA.
     Our work was conducted in the macrotidal, salt-marsh-dominated Plum Island Sound Long-Term Ecological Research Site on the Gulf of Maine coast of northeastern Massachusetts. Freshwater input is delivered mainly by the Parker River from a mixed forest-suburban watershed. The estuary is shallow and vertically well mixed, with a persistent horizontal salinity gradient from tidal-fresh and oligohaline reaches upstream to polyhaline waters near the barrier inlet. Extensive Spartina alterniflora and S. patens marshes, dense tidal-creek networks, and broad intertidal flats promote strong pelagic-benthic coupling. Tidal range is large (2–3 m), residence times are short (days), and interannual hydrography is shaped by river discharge, storms and nor’easters, and marsh flooding. For analysis we stratified the system into upper, middle, and lower regions that capture this hydrogeomorphic gradient, from freshwater-influenced creeks to marine-dominated open waters. Long-term PIE LTER instrumentation (continuous water-quality sensors, discharge gauges) and standardized biological sampling provide the multi-decadal context and consistency required for year-to-year comparisons.
Estuary hydrography
     We analyzed continuous sonde hydrography from two PIE LTER stations representing the lower estuary (IBYC) and middle estuary (OTL) for 2000–2025. For Figure 2, we extracted temperature and conductivity observations recorded during high-tide periods and summarized data by three growing-season windows (spring, summer, fall; Season 1–3 in the sonde table). Data were harmonized by station and year, date-time fields were parsed to local time, and only finite-valued records were retained. No additional outlier removal was applied. For each station and season, anomalies were defined as observed values minus that station’s long-term seasonal mean. Seasonal anomalies were then aggregated by year and region (lower, middle) for trend estimation and visualization.

Trend estimation
     To quantify long-term change in temperature and conductivity or salinity, we estimated trends with Yue-Pilon prewhitened Sen slopes and tested monotonic change with Mann-Kendall statistics to account for temporal autocorrelation. We report trend magnitudes by estuarine region and control multiple comparisons with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction; inferential results are reported as FDR-adjusted q-values.
Crab burrow density
     To quantify the density of mud fiddler crabs, we conducted annual quadrat surveys in six marshes during the growing season (May–September) from 2014–2025. At each marsh, we established a 200 m transect parallel to a tidal creek and selected 20 points along that transect using a random number generator. At each point along the transect, we counted the burrows of adult crabs (>1.0 cm) in a 0.0625 m2 quadrat within 0.5 m of the marsh edge. Site metadata (creek identity, bank or branch, habitat, reach type) and plot distances were recorded at each visit.
     We estimated the long-term multiplicative trend in annual density using a log-linear model fit to the annual across-creek medians, reporting the percent change per year and 95% confidence intervals from the model’s slope estimate. This approach treats interannual change as approximately proportional on the original scale while retaining zeros via the transformation. The log-linear test and the non-parametric Kendall trend test were Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted and are reported as q-values.

Food web analysis
     We used the PIE LTER long-term stable isotope food-web functional group data set spanning the three estuarine regions (upper, middle, and lower). For the native-consumer annual trend analysis in Fig. 4, the packaged time series runs from 2009–2020. All sampling and mixing-model protocols follow Nelson et al. 2015^24. Sampling is conducted between August and September annually to capture the production sources integrated over the spring and summer growing season in PIE. We assessed change in marine plankton (POM) contribution over time by fitting linear models to annual POM contribution values summarized by region (food-web median across native focal species) and by species, and applied Benjamini-Hochberg correction across regional and species trend tests (reported as q-values).
     We refer to this source as marine plankton (POM) because it is the particulate organic matter or planktonic end-member used in the PIE mixing framework. Operationally, this source represents suspended planktonic organic matter available to consumers within the estuary and should not be interpreted as direct evidence that adult mud fiddler crabs feed by filtering water-column phytoplankton. Source separation in the mixing model is aided by the two-isotope structure of the end-members, especially the strong d34S contrast among POM, Spartina marsh grass, and benthic microalgae in the PIE source set. Because plankton isotope values can vary along estuarine salinity gradients, we interpret temporal change conservatively as increased support from a planktonic POM pathway rather than proof of a single offshore source.
     For the invading mud fiddler crab, we applied the Bayesian isotope mixing model (MixSIAR) to 20 individuals sampled in 2023 and 2025 from the lower estuary to estimate source support using the same two-isotope system (d13C and d34S) and three primary sources (benthic microalgae, marine plankton (POM), and Spartina marsh grass) with trophic enrichment factors derived from PIE reference species.
